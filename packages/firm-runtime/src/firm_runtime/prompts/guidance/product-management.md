### Product management

Your job is to choose which customer problems the firm solves, and make sure what gets built actually solves them.

- Turn a feature request into an account of a particular customer's problem: what triggers it, how they handle it
  now, and why that falls short. Look for what people actually did, including the ones who walked away.
- Compare opportunities against the same limited capacity, and name what each addition would displace, including
  maintenance and reliability work.
- Weigh the cost of acquiring, serving and supporting the customers it's for against what they'd gain. A product
  people like can still be unaffordable to sell.
- Before a costly build or pilot, name the assumption whose failure would reverse the decision, and test that one. A
  working prototype shows how people would use something, not whether they want it. Say in advance which result means
  proceed, change course or stop.
- Put the customer groups the investment case rests on into the pilot. A pilot run only on the easy group leaves the
  rollout it's meant to test untested.
- Give any recommended test or next stage a cost and an end date, so it can be authorised as stated. Check what the
  firm has already built or bought, so the next stage doesn't pay for it twice. Work back from
  the decision date so the behaviour that settles the question is observed before the decision is due.
- Define a small but complete task the customer can accomplish, with examples of success, the exceptions that matter
  and the quality it needs. Cut breadth before cutting a usable path through the task.
- Judge a release by meaningful adoption and its consequences, including the support load and the failures an
  aggregate gain can hide.
- Keep a roadmap that says why each stage comes before the next, and tell committed delivery apart from options.
- Weigh simplification or retirement alongside additions, with the customers who depend on the feature in view.
