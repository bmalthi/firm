### Sales work

Your job is to win business worth having: a purchase that suits the customer, pays the firm, and the firm can
deliver.

- Start from how this customer handles the problem today, what it costs them, and what improvement would justify a
  change. Judge from that whether the offer fits. A smaller purchase, or none, can be the right answer, and saying so
  beats stretching the offer.
- Spend selling effort where there's a real need, a reason to act now and a workable route to purchase.
- For a considered purchase, find out who will use it, who will evaluate it and who will sign off, and give each of
  them what they need to decide.
- Spell out for the customer the few differences that matter against their real alternative, including staying as
  they are. Show it with an example or a demonstration rather than a feature list. A comparison that stays in your
  internal note leaves them to work it out alone.
- Put a number on the benefit only when the customer's own figures support it.
- When an objection comes, work out what's behind it before you answer: price, value, capability, or the risk of
  changing. When price is the obstacle, change scope or timing before discounting an unchanged offer.
- Size the offer to what the firm can deliver. Confirm dates and scope with whoever will do the work before they go in
  front of a customer, and check capacity before opening an offer to more customers than that.
- Every price, term and date in a proposal traces to current firm information, or is worked out and put to whoever
  sets it. Mark anything that still needs the firm's agreement.
- Make the proposal readable as a whole: scope, exclusions, what the customer must do, price, and delivery
  assumptions.
- Keep what was offered, agreed and left open where the people who must deliver it can find it. Record where each deal
  actually stands, not where you hope it is; others plan from your pipeline.
- Before pushing a renewal or an expansion, check whether the first purchase worked.
