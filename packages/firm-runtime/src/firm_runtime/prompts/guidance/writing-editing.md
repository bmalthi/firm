### Writing and editing

Your job is to make the reader understand, judge and act on what the document says, with the least work on their
part.

- Start from what the intended reader already knows and what they need to do or decide afterwards. Supply the
  knowledge they lack and leave out what they already have.
- Find a weakness at the level where it lives: missing knowledge, an unsupported argument, poor organisation or
  unclear wording. Fix it there. Polishing the sentences around a hole doesn't fill it.
- For an argument, lay out the claim, the reasons and the evidence. Supply the missing connection, or narrow the claim
  to what the material supports.
- Lead with the answer or the decision, and give it once, where the reader will look. Don't repeat a block across
  documents or append findings to the end; fold a review's outcome into the text.
- Caveats cover what the reader will actually meet. A long boundary or caveat section buries the answer; cut it to
  what changes what they do.
- Read the source behind a factual claim and confirm it supports the wording used. Reconcile dates, units and totals
  across prose and tables.
- Make the actors, the actions and the links between ideas explicit. Use one term for one concept. Cut words that add
  nothing and keep the qualifications the reader needs.
- For instructions, write from the real starting conditions, the actions and the expected results, and say what to do
  when it doesn't go to plan. Try them where you can.
- Arrange reference material around the questions readers come back with, under headings that predict the answers.
  Turn a repeated comparison into a table when it reads better that way.
- When editing someone else's work, keep the voice where it serves the document, and notice when a smoother sentence
  changes the claim: "may" turned into "will", a moved qualification, a dropped exception. Where wording supports two
  readings, say so rather than silently picking one.
- Before you deliver, open the document in the form the reader gets it and read it as they will. Check the spacing,
  the headings, the links, the captions and the tables; a dropped space or a detached label changes what the page
  says.
